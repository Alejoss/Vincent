01 — The City's Answer

## The City Gave Us Answers. Here's What They Left Out.

In 2026 the Corona Police Department released a Flock "Sharing, Access & Audit Controls" FAQ. We read it closely. Several of its reassurances don't survive the public record — and the most important one is something the FAQ openly admits.

The City Says

"Federal sharing is completely disabled in California due to state law."

What They Left Out

"Disabled" is a **setting**, not a guarantee. In Oxnard and Ventura County that exact setting was silently overridden by Flock's own back end — Ventura County had "National Lookup" off since June 2023, yet a vendor configuration error produced **364,000 out-of-state queries** without the agency's knowledge. And a federal subpoena or National Security Letter served on Flock's corporate HQ bypasses the setting entirely — Flock may be legally barred from even telling Corona PD it happened.

The City Says

"Audit logs are comprehensive and tamper-resistant… monthly audit records are classified as investigative documents and may not be available in response to public records requests."

What They Left Out

This is the whole problem. **An audit no one outside the department can see is not accountability — it's "trust us."** Under CLETS, misuse must be reported to the California Attorney General and is subject to public oversight. If Corona's controls are as strong as the FAQ claims, there is no reason not to publish the audits — or independent summaries of them.

The City Says

"Cameras only capture vehicles and license plates — no race, gender, height, or facial recognition."

What They Left Out

This conflates what the city **pays to view** (plate data) with what the Flock system **captures and classifies** on its back end. Flock markets "Vehicle Fingerprint" — make, model, color, type, and attributes like roof racks and bumper stickers — generated by machine learning *without* a plate. That data already exists in Flock's database; the city could enable more of it for a fee, and legal process to Flock could reach it. "We only look at plates" is not "the system only captures plates."

The City Says

"We conduct regular audits of Flock inquiries to maintain compliance."

What They Left Out

These are **self-audits the public can't verify**, and in network audits the sensitive fields from outside agencies are masked *even from Corona PD*. It was Flock's own vendor audit — not the city — that found **203 inactive user accounts** with access. "Regular audits" with no independent review and no public disclosure is exactly the gap CLETS was built to close.

The City Says

"Other agencies cannot access our data without our knowledge, and access can be revoked at any time."

What They Left Out

Ventura County's 364,000 out-of-state queries happened **without the agency's knowledge**, through a change on the vendor's side. Being able to revoke access doesn't help when you were never told the access occurred.

**Notice what the FAQ concedes:** the audits exist, but they're classified so the public can't see them. That single admission is the case for this entire page. California already built a license-plate system that doesn't get to say that — it's called CLETS.

Source: City of Corona "Flock Sharing, Access & Audit Controls FAQ" and "Corona Police Department Policy," 2026; Oxnard PD press release 2/27/26; Ventura County Sheriff audit findings; Flock Safety "Vehicle Fingerprint" product materials.

02 — One Standard

The accountability we're asking for isn't hypothetical — California has run it for decades. CLETS, the state's license-plate and criminal-justice network, is governed by statute, enforced by felony penalties, and subject to mandatory public audits. Corona chose a private vendor with none of those guardrails. Here is the gap, line by line.

03 — The Ask

## What Real Accountability Looks Like

This coalition started when a parent with 20 years of IT-security experience noticed Flock cameras lining the route from home to their children's school — logging the family into a corporate database every morning. The fix isn't complicated, and it isn't anti-police. **It's the same accountability California already requires of CLETS.**

We are asking the City of Corona to bring its Flock surveillance program up to that standard — and to put it in writing, in the binding contract, where a vendor's revocable policy can't quietly change it.

Each ask maps directly to a row in the comparison above: a guardrail CLETS has, and Flock currently doesn't.

**If the city will not meet this bar,** then it is running mass surveillance on every resident with less oversight than a 40-year-old state system — and the only responsible option left is to take it down.

### Our Five Asks

- **Publish the audits.** Release the monthly Flock audit records — or independent summaries. No "investigative document" exemption for a system that scans everyone.
- **Put retention in the contract.** Write the deletion schedule into the binding Professional Services Agreement, not Flock's revocable policy.
- **Real penalties for misuse.** Contractual consequences for unauthorized access — not a vendor press release after the fact.
- **Council oversight of every change.** A public council vote and city-attorney review before any setting, sharing, scope, or contract change.
- **Independent access audit.** A third-party audit — not a vendor self-audit — of all federal and out-of-state queries against Corona's data.

04 — The Issue

## Why We Don't Take "Trust Us" at Face Value

Flock Safety markets itself as a crime-fighting tool. What it actually is: a private company building a permanent, city-wide record of every resident's daily movements — then monetizing access to that data.

Every Flock camera photographs passing vehicles and transmits plate numbers, timestamps, GPS locations, and travel directions to Flock's corporate cloud. That data is not stored by the Corona Police Department. **It is stored by a private company, on private servers, under Flock's terms — not ours.**

Residents have no rights under California public records law against a private company's database. The city handed Flock a surveillance monopoly on our streets and gave up control of what happens next.

The documented record shows exactly what happens next: federal agencies get access. Sanctuary protections get bypassed. And Flock's own CEO admitted the company enabled national federal access to cameras without the knowledge of the cities that paid for them.

**This is not about fighting crime.** A targeted warrant for a specific suspect's plate history is one thing. Photographing every vehicle entering and leaving Corona — every day, indefinitely — is a corporate surveillance operation. These are not the same.

05 — The Infrastructure

## The Surveillance Pipeline

Flock cameras are the data-collection layer of a broader surveillance infrastructure designed to feed into Palantir's analytics platform — which connects to federal law enforcement and immigration systems.

Palantir holds a $30 million contract with ICE to consolidate sensitive personal data — biometrics, geolocation, personal identifiers — for near real-time immigration enforcement. **Flock is the front door to that system.**

Oxnard PD set their Flock portal to "California only" — then discovered federal agencies had been searching their data anyway via a vendor-enabled back-end override. 5 million queries on just 19 cameras in 2025. Oxnard suspended Flock in February 2026. The "California only" setting was theater.

**The question isn't whether this data could be misused.** The record from San Francisco, Mountain View, and 22 states shows it already has been. The question is whether Corona wants to remain part of this infrastructure.
